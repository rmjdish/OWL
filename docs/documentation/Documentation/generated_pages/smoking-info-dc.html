Overview

Smoking (ages 20 to 60+ years) Information on smoking tobacco was obtained across the entire life course in the NSHD at ages 20, 25, 31, 36, 43, 53 and 60+ years. From age 36 onwards, information was obtained during a home-interview conducted by research nurses, while data at earlier years were collected by postal questionnaires. Cohort members who provided an affirmative response to being a current cigarette smoker were classified as ‘smokers’, regardless of the quantity of cigarettes smoked per day, while those who provided a negative response were classified as ‘non-smokers’. More information on each of these variables is presented below.

Method:

Smoking status and frequency Participants who were smokers were asked the number of cigarettes smoked per day. They were also asked the age at which they started smoking and, if ex-smokers, the age when they stopped. Using this information, smoking status at age 36 was classified as current, ex, or never.
Pack years The number of pack years per person represents the average number of cigarettes smoked per day divided by 20 (the number of cigarettes in a standard pack), multiplied by the number of years an individual has been smoking. For example, one cigarette pack-year represents smoking one pack of cigarettes a day for one year (or half a pack a day for two years). In the NSHD, the pack-years variable was first derived by (Strand et al., 2011) using data on smoking frequency from the first data collection at age 20 up to 53. a new pack-years variable was derived for up to 63 years. This was constructed by summing data from 7 study periods: 20 years and younger, 20–25 years, 25–31 years, 31–36 years, 36–43 years, 43-53 years and 53-63 years. Participants who never smoked were assigned a pack-years value of zero, those with scores up to ten pack years a value of one, those with scores from ten to 20 pack years a value of two and those with a score higher than 20 pack years a value of three.

Literature Review

Previous results from the NSHD provide substantial evidence for the assertion that smoking is associated with greater cognitive decline in a variety of cognitive domains such as verbal memory and visual search speed. The results of this study showed that the associations with a faster decline in verbal memory and visual search speed between 43 and 53 years were mainly confined to heavy smoking (those smoking more than 20 cigarettes per day) (Richards et al., 2003). In this analysis, the authors classified cigarette-smoking frequency at age 43 as none, one to 20 cigarettes per day, and more than 20 cigarettes per day. Verbal memory was assessed by a 15-item word-learning task in which the participants were shown each word for two seconds, and then asked to write down all they could remember. The total score over three trials was recorded and the words were changed between assessments at 43 and 53 years, in order to avoid practice effects. Visual search speed consisted in the search for two letters from the alphabet ‘p’ and ‘w’ embedded in a grille of other alphabetical letters in a timed (one-minute) test. Participants who smoked at every assessment in midlife had significantly lower scores in verbal memory and visual search at both 43 and 53 years and a greater decline in both cognitive tests. However, the association between smoking and memory was not observed at age 43, rather there was a strong association with a change in memory over ten years. Moreover, Richards and colleagues showed that long-term ex-smokers at age 53 (which meant NSHD participants who had stopped smoking after the age of 36 years) had better memory scores at age 53 and a slower memory decline from 43 to 53 years compared with never-smokers.
