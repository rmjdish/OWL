1 Executive summary

1.1 Introduction

Assessment of dietary intake of cohort members has been an important component of

the MRC National Survey of Health and Development (NSHD), particularly in adulthood.

The clinic visit or having a home visit conducted in 2006-11 has enabled a fifth

assessment of diet, the earlier timepoints being at age 4 years in 1950, at age 36 years

in 1982, 43 years in 1989 and 53 years in 1999. These repeated measures of diet

enable trends in intakes to be examined and relationships to health measures to be

explored, of particular interest now that the cohort is reaching its ageing years.

1.2 Aim of report

This report outlines the procedures used to collect, code and analyse dietary information

from the assessment of NSHD cohort members at age 60-64 years. It describes the

consumption of foods and food groups and intake of nutrients for the 2006-11 timepoint

and relates the intakes of macronutrients to dietary goals and of micronutrients to

Dietary Reference Values (DRVs). It also compares the intakes in 2006-11 to the survey

carried out in 1999 to provided indications of how the diets of the cohort members have

changed over the intervening 7-10 year period.

1.3 Methods

Dietary intakes were collected as 5 day estimated (unweighed) diaries, as they were in

the previous adult assessments, including two weekend days if possible. Diaries were

given to cohort members at the end of the clinic visit, with nurses giving detailed

instructions on completion of the diary and the level of detail required. Cohort members

sent the completed diaries directly to HNR in prepaid envelopes; these were logged on

arrival. Diaries were coded and edited by HNR’s dietary assessment team, using DINO

(Data In Nutrients Out), HNR’s purpose built Access based dietary assessment system,

incorporating food composition tables from McCance and Widdowson's The Composition

of Foods 6th edition. Resulting food and nutrient intakes were averaged for the days of

collection and reported as mean intakes for 5 days. Results were compared, where

possible, to those from the 1999 assessment, statistical analysis being carried by

ANOVA.

1.4 Results

1869 cohort members (880M, 989F) completed at least 3 days of diet record, 84% of

those attending the clinic visit. Of these 1318 (70.5%) (594M, 724F) also completed at

least 3 days in 1999.

For those foods that can be compared to 1999 (some food groupings were different

between the two timepoints), consumption of white bread was lower and granary and

wholemeal higher in 2006-11 than in 1999, and there was a large reduction in

consumption of cakes and sweet puddings. Milk consumption decreased and was

predominantly semi-skimmed; cheese consumption was unchanged but yoghurts

increased. Consumption of both red and white meats was reduced from 1999, but there

was no change in fish. Vegetable consumption was higher, but potatoes lower. Coffee

consumption was lower.

In terms of energy and nutrients, energy intakes were somewhat lower in 2006-11

compared to 1999 and represented 88-89% of the Average Energy Requirement (AER)

for this age group for the UK. Energy intake has been found to be less than energy

expenditure as measured by doubly-labelled water when assessed using dietary records,

as it is with other detailed methods of dietary assessment, such as 24 hour recall, but

here is no indication that the degree of underestimation has changed from previous

. Intakes of total fat were little changed since 1999,

but saturated fat intakes were reduced, with a lower percentage of individuals above the

recommended 10% of energy in 2006-11 than in 1999. Intakes of non-starch

polysaccharide (NSP) (dietary fibre) were higher than in 1999. There was no difference

in alcohol consumption, although the types of drinks consumed were slightly different,

with increases seen for wine. Intakes of vitamins were higher in some cases and overall

the percentages of cohort members with intakes below the Lower Reference Nutrient

Intake (LRNI) values were lower than in 1999. For minerals, there were few changes,

and the percentage of individuals with intakes of potassium and zinc below the LRNI was

increased compared to 1999.

A successful assessment of dietary intake of the NSHD cohort members was carried out

as part of the clinic visit at age 60-64 years from 2006-11, with good responses rates to

the request to complete the diet diary and well completed diaries, which allow prompt

and complete coding and editing, and providing a complete dietary dataset by the

Summer of 2011. Dietary data indicate that food and nutrient intakes are largely similar

to those of 1999, with small decreases in energy intakes, which would be expected for

this age group compared to earlier adulthood. Trends from 1999 indicate that dietary

2 Introduction

2.1 Background

The MRC National Survey of Health and Development (NSHD), or the 1946 British birth

cohort is the longest running birth cohort in the world, with data collected on numerous

occasions since the birth of cohort members in one week of March 1946.

Dietary data had been collected at ages 4, 36, 43 and 53 years. At age 4 years, a brief

24 hour dietary recall was conducted to mothers of the cohort members. In adulthood,

an estimated (unweighed) diet diary was used. At ages 36 and 43 years, this consisted

of 2 days of recall and 5 days of prospective recording; at age 53 years only the 5 days

of prospective recording was collected, and in general the 5 days of prospective

recording have been used in publications of dietary data from all adult timepoints much

more frequently than the 2 days of recall.

The first adult assessment in 1982 was coordinated and administered by NSHD in Bristol.

A 5 day estimated (unweighed) diary was used with an interviewer assisted recall of the

2 days immediately preceding the interview. The diary, which asked for information on

portion sizes to be given in household measures, was adapted from the weighed food

assessments used in other studies. The dietary information was converted into food

codes and weights by manual coding before being converted into electronic form for

calculation of nutrient intakes. In the mid-1980s, the Dunn Nutrition Unit in Cambridge

became more involved with the dietary component of NSHD and at the next assessment

in 1989, the diary was modified to include some photographs to assist portion size

estimations. A computer program, DIDO (Diet In and Data Out), designed in

Cambridge, was used to enter food data directly; the output files being compatible with

the Dunn’s in–house nutrient analysis

to record dietary intakes. With the increased recognition of how eating environment

may influence the quantity and quality of food consumed and hence nutrient intake,

extra information was collected about characteristics associated with each eating

occasion.

3 Methodology

3.1 Background

Dietary data were collected as part of the clinic visit for the NSHD assessment at age 60-

64 years. Consistent with previous method of assessment, dietary intakes were

evaluated using a 5-day estimated (unweighed) diet diary. The diet diary used for this

data collection was the current HNR diary; this had been modified from the previous

assessments of the cohort to yield information on where foods were eaten and who was

present during the eating occasion.

3.2 Placing the diet diary

The diet diary was placed towards the end of the clinic or home visit. Prior to the

appointment, it was determined whether or not each cohort member had completed a

diary in 1999, as this would affect the level of discussion and detail needed for

completion of the diary when it was placed. Cohort members were asked to record 5

days of dietary intake for the next 5 days, i.e. beginning the day after the clinic visit , or

if not the next day, at least within the next week. Each diary was provided in a zip lock

plastic wallet for the collection of package labels. This also included the stamped

envelope for the return of the completed diary to HNR. A flyer explaining the reasons

why dietary information was collected was also given to cohort members to encourage

participation (Appendix A). Each diet diary and zip-lock plastic wallet was labelled with

the HNR number, which was created at HNR to enable identification of NHS numbers and

cohort members’ names for correspondence regarding blood samples. Since dietary

data throughout the survey are identified by NSHD serial number, the HNR number was

later converted to serial number for each diary. The appointment date was also

recorded inside the front cover of the diary as a second measure of identification.

The nurses who handed out the diaries had been trained in the method of diet recording

and explained the procedure to the cohort members. A completed example of a day’s

food record was provided at the beginning of the diary. Each day was separated into

seven time slots for entry and cohort members were also asked to record the exact time

of each eating occasion. The nurses demonstrated the various ways that portion size

could be estimated:

 in household measures (e.g. one tablespoon of baked beans, one Kit Kat (two

fingers)

 for packaged foods to note the weight indicated on the packet

 photographs provided in the diary for 15 frequently consumed foods as small,

medium and large portion sizes which cohort members could use for identical or

similar foods

8

For each eating occasion, cohort members were asked to record where they were and

who else was present at the time of eating.

At the end of each day there was space to record ingredients for recipes of dishes made

at home by the cohort member and also space for any dietary supplements taken that

day (Appendix C). Attention was also drawn to the checklist at the end of the diary

where cohort members were asked to record their usual eating habits (for example, type

of milk or fat spread usually consumed) to facilitate coding in cases where details were

omitted in the diet record. They were asked to record brand names for foods wherever

possible and were asked to collect the food label information/wrappers for any unusual

foods and ready meals consumed to help coders identify or clarify items.

3.3 Tracking the diet diary

All cohort members, seen at the clinics or at home, were asked to complete a diet diary.

Initially, nurses were asked to notify HNR whenever a diet diary was taken by a cohort

member via the tracking documents that were faxed to HNR. However, this proved to

be challenging and led to a change in protocol in November 2010 where HNR would only

be notified if the cohort member declined to participate in the dietary assessment.

Nurses were instructed to record this information explicitly on either the fax cover sheet

or the Blood Sample Tracking form, where information was entered into the NSHD

database along with other tracking data by the HNR data entry team.

The completed diet diary was returned to HNR by post in a pre-paid envelope given to

the cohort member during their clinic or home visit. Once received, HNR personnel

logged the diet diary firstly into the NSHD database using the HNR number attached to

the diary; date of receipt, the numbers of diary days completed, the numbers of food

packages returned and whether or not the general questions on the last pages of the

diary were completed, were then entered to this database. This diary was subsequently

relabelled with the corresponding NSHD serial number, which is a unique number given

to the cohort member at birth, when they were enrolled into the study. The task of

converting the numbers on the diet diaries from the HNR number to the serial number

was originally carried out by LHA staff; this was subsequently taken over by HNR

personnel. The relabelled diaries were logged onto the HNR in-house dietary assessment

10

system DINO (Diet-In-Nutrients-Out) where each step of data entry was tracked (i.e.

coding and editing).

3.4 Dietary data processing

Once the received diaries were logged at HNR, they were passed to HNR’s dietary

assessment team to be coded by trained coders and editors. Food intakes were entered

into a modified version of HNR’s dietary assessment system DINO (Diet In Nutrients

Out), an all-in-one dietary recording and analysis system written in Microsoft Access.

DINO is an updated system from the DIDO system used previously; it is based on the

same principles as DIDO, but is more versatile and user friendly. The food composition

data used was based on McCance and Widdowson’s “The Composition of Foods”, 6th

edition2 with additions where necessary for new foods (see below).

Coders attempted to match each food or drink item recorded in the diary with a food

code and a portion code from DINO. For composite items, which could be split into their

component parts, for example sandwiches, each individual component was assigned. If

an item had been recorded and there was no suitable code in DINO or there was

insufficient detail to code the food, the entry was flagged as a query.

Within DINO, each food code is linked to appropriate portion size descriptors, mainly

household measures, which are then linked to the correct weight for that portion or,

where the portion size is described as a weight, the weight can be entered directly in

grams. The coding of portions described as small, medium or large was based on the

Food Standards Agency’s reference book “Food Portion Sizes”

3

. Weights for common

branded foods from supermarkets and manufacturers and for foods from fast food

outlets were obtained from the relevant website or if necessary, a food would be

purchased and weighed.

Where the coder could not resolve the food or portion consumed, the entry was flagged

as a query for action by an editor who had greater nutrition background and experience.

The editors assigned appropriate codes for all flagged food and portion codes and

checked any other queries raised by the coders. In general, where details for the coding

of foods were missing, formally agreed default codes were used, such as for the type of

milk in tea or coffee in a café, or lasagne eaten at a restaurant. Where portion sizes

were missing, an estimate was made using the same weight if the food was consumed

For a new product not in DINO, editors visited supermarkets or contacted the

Where a cohort member consumed a homemade recipe, each individual food ingredient

was linked with the food group of the recipe. DINO has the capability to report on these

foods both at the recipe level and food level, providing a clearer picture of consumption

of components like meat, fish, fruit and vegetables.

For a homemade dish where a recipe had been recorded, the ingredients were entered

individually using the appropriate cooked food codes, and all the codes for the dish were

allocated to a recipe food group according to the type of dish. The weight of each

cooked ingredient was calculated using the raw weight recorded by the cohort member,

a weight loss factor for the whole dish (from a comparable recipe in McCance and

Where the food was stated as homemade but there was no recipe given, a standard

homemade recipe food code was chosen.

3.5 Quality control

3.5.1 Training

HNR was responsible for providing dietary assessment training for clinic staff, who were

trained prior to the start of visits with cohort members. HNR personnel demonstrated to

the nurses how and where to label the diary and the accompanying zip-lock plastic

wallet, where to record the appointment date, and how to use the examples and the

photographs of portion sizes in the front of the diary to help the cohort members to

complete the 5-day record. Emphasis was also placed on the importance of asking the

cohort members to provide as much detail as possible about their intake, as well as

asking them not to change their usual diet during the recording period. Nurses were

12

instructed to encourage the cohort members to keep the diary about food and drink

consumed for 3 weekdays and 2 weekend days.

3.5.2 Dietary assessment

At the start of the coding process, editors checked ten complete diaries for each coder

and gave them individual feedback on their work. For a random 10% of all diaries the

editors undertook a further 100% check of all food and portion code entries. This

ensured that error rates were monitored for all the coders working on the project and

helped identify any coding issues. An error rate greater than 5% from any one coder

resulted in further 100% checks of their coding until the editor was satisfied that

problems had been eliminated. All of the entries flagged as queries by the coders were

categorised into eight query types, such as food code or portion code not available in

At the end of coding and editing, each cohort member’s energy and nutrient intake for

identified as being either above or below the normal distribution were identified and the

original diary entries checked against the coding. Portion sizes were also examined by

food group for outliers and those identified checked against diary entries.

4.3.1 Cereals and cereal products

The subgroup of cereal and cereal products with the highest average consumption was

white bread, which also had the greatest percentage of consumers in this group at

78.6% of men and 72.6% of women. Consumption of white bread was reduced from the

1999 assessment and there was a small increase in consumption of brown/granary and

wheatgerm breads. Women ate less wholemeal bread than in 1999 but there was no

change in men although nearly half of all cohort members consumed some wholemeal

bread in 2006-11. Rice was consumed by around 40% of cohort members; there was no

change in the average weight consumed from 1999. The weight of pasta consumed was

around half of that reported in 1999 (from 36.7g/d to 19.3g/d in men and from 33.0g/d

to 16.0g/d in women), but this was most likely due to pasta dishes being grouped

differently at that assessment. For example, lasagne was coded as a pasta dish in 1999;

however, this has been revised and further differentiated in 2006-11 to improve the

precision of classifying composite dishes such that lasagne was by default coded as

“beef, veal and dishes”, pork lasagne was coded as “pork and dishes” and vegetable

lasagne coded as “other vegetables”. It is only when the recipe was available in the diet

diary that the lasagne would be coded as “pasta and pasta dishes”.

Oat based and high fibre breakfast cereals were consumed in greater quantities and by

more participants than low fibre breakfast cereals. There was a large reduction in the

consumption of cakes and sweet puddings since 1999 but biscuit consumption was not

changed. A new product since 1999, cereal bars, was consumed in greater quantities

than other sweet cereal products such as cakes and biscuits and by a high proportion of
