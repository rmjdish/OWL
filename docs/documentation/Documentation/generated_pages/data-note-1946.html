Job and occupational histories for the NSHD 1946 Birth Cohort

Erzsébet Bukodi and Jenny Neuburger September 2009.

Acknowledgements: This work forms part of the ESRC Gender Network Grant, Project 1 ‘Changing occupational careers of men and women’, Reference: RES-225-25-2001. 1. Introduction This Note contains a detailed description of a dataset containing occupational histories for members of the 1946 Cohort Study. It also describes the original variables used and the work done to construct this dataset.
The dataset includes information on jobs reported by cohort members at one of eight surveys conducted between the ages of 16 and 53. The jobs are organised into chronological order using reported or inferred job start dates in conjunction with the interview dates when the job episodes were collected. Information on the occupation of each job has been re-coded from SOC60, SOC70 and SOC80 codes into SOC90 codes. The dataset is designed for the analysis of changes in occupational status over time. It does not include information on spells of unemployment or economic inactivity. Table 1 gives an overview of the original survey data used to construct the occupational histories. Table A1 in the Appendix provides a detailed description of variables used. From Table 1 it is clear that the histories are not complete for all cohort members. The main reasons for gaps in the job histories include: Information on job start dates and occupation codes available electronically only for men for period 1965-72. Unit non-response to one of the surveys. Item non-response to job and occupation questions. Held more jobs than asked about over a given period.

The rest of the Data Note is organised as follows:

1) Approach to organising the job histories. 2) Imputation of missing job start and end dates. 3) Creating an event-history data-file. 4) Recoding occupational data. 5) Constructing social class classifications. 6) Description of final dataset.

2. Approach to organising the job histories The job histories are constructed by piecing together the key information from each of the eight surveys. Initially, eight separate job history sections were identified, corresponding to periods up to and between the eight surveys. Information collected at each of the eight surveys potentially contributes one or more jobs to the job history. In total, an individual’s job history can contain information on up to a maximum of fifteen jobs held between the ages of 16 and 53 (see Table A1 in the Appendix).
First, the original variables were collected into a single data file, including information on job start dates, the total number of jobs held between one survey and the next, detailed occupational codes, codes for socio-economic groups where available, self-employment status and full-time or part-time status. Second, key’ jobs were identified as those for which SOC codes were collected. For thesekey’ jobs, job start dates were imputed where these were missing or incomplete, using additional information about length of service in a job or end dates of previous jobs or spells of unemployment. The rules and the information used to replace missing values are outlined in the next part of this Data Note. Third, the jobs were organised into chronological order using the reported or imputed job start dates. Where information about job start dates collected at different surveys overlapped or conflicted, the information collected nearest in time to the job in question was used. Where a cohort member had not changed job between one survey and the next and where updated SOC codes were collected, all SOC codes were included for the job. For example, a cohort member who remained in the same job between 1964 and 1977 will have had their occupation coded twice; once to the 1960 classification (SOC60) and once to the 1970 classification (SOC70).

3. Imputation of missing job start and end dates The following rules and additional information have been used to impute missing job start dates: Use June as the start month where the start year for a job is given, but the start month is missing Use the start date of the job reported in a previous interview when there has been no change of job Use the interview date minus the length of time spent in a current job, where the start date is not asked for but duration of time spent in a current job is asked Use the end date for a job held immediately before the job in question (asked in the 1982 survey only) Use the interview date minus a period calculated by dividing the length of time since the previous interview by the total number of jobs held over that period The availability of this additional information varies from survey to survey. For example, at age 53, cohort members were not asked when they had started their current job or how long they had been in their current job. Job start dates were imputed by dividing the ten-year period since the last survey (1989-99) by the number of paid jobs a cohort member had held over that period i.e. if three jobs were held over this period then the imputed start date for a current job was three years ago, in 1996. A detailed description of imputation rules used at each survey is given in Table A2 in the Appendix. End dates for jobs were collected in the 1982 survey for up to six previous jobs, and in the 1989 survey for the most recently held job (if not currently in work) and/or for the longest job held between 1982 and 1989 (if the current job was not the longest job held). For these jobs, job end months were imputed as June if the job end year was reported, but the end month was missing. Where information on a job end year and a job end month was not collected, it was not imputed.

4. Creating an event-history data-file In the next step, we created an event-history data-file of job histories. In an event-history data-set each row (record) of the file is related to time duration of an episode and each record contains all the information on the job history of a cohort member, from leaving school to age 53. It was therefore necessary to reshape the data so that each record corresponds to a job episode, and consecutive jobs of a cohort member’s career are stored successively in the file. 5. Recoding occupational data Occupational data are routinely classified into the official national Standard Occupational Classification (SOC). This schema has been revised in the first year of every decade since 1960. Thus, across the cohort sweeps of the 1946 Birth Cohort Study, occupations were coded to changing official classifications. It was therefore necessary to re-code occupations throughout the job histories in a consistent way. We re-coded all occupational codes into the SOC90 classification. Since a direct recoding of SOC60 and SOC70 into SOC90 was not feasible, in the first step we re-coded occupational data in SOC60 into SOC70. The SOC60 and SOC70 classifications are essentially the same, with only a few cases of groups being split or combined. Table A3 in the Appendix shows the link between these two classifications. In the second step we re-coded occupational data in SOC70 into SOC80. Table A4 in the Appendix shows the cross-walk. In the final step we re-coded occupational data in SOC80 into SOC90. Table A5 in the Appendix shows the link between SOC90 and SOC80 occupational categories. Although there is not a one-to-one correspondence between occupational codes, the match is quite close. We implemented a simple check to examine whether or not the re-coding across different occupational classifications yielded significant bias in cohort members’ occupational distributions. For this purpose, we recorded cohort members’ occupations at age 40, 45 and 50, using SOC90 major categories, and compared the occupational distributions at these ages with those derived from the 1991 Census. In order to make comparisons with the 1991 Census, we had to take two fairly broad age ranges of people: men aged 30-44 and 45-64 years old, and women who were between 30 and 44 and 45 and 59 years in 1991. Tables 4 and 5 show the occupational distributions separately for men and women. The figures suggest that the three rounds of occupational re-coding of 1946 male and female cohort members’ occupations has produced distributions across SOC90 occupational codes that are quite similar to those produced from the 1991 Census for overlapping age ranges.

6. Constructing social class classifications The final event-history dataset also contains variables on cohort members’ social class positions throughout their working lives. For each job spell of a cohort member we have constructed two classifications of social class: the Goldthorpe class schema and the National Statistics Socio-Economic Classification (NS-SEC) (see: ). Three versions of each of the classification have been created: The full version [GOLDCLASS_FULL, NSSEC_FULL] which derived from information on detailed occupation as coded into SOC90 classification (using the 3-digit codes); employment status distinguishing four categories: employers with 25 or more employees, employers less than 25 employees, self-employed and employees; in the case of employees, managerial/supervisory position distinguishing the following categories: manager in large establishment, manager in small establishment and supervisor. When information on either employment status or managerial/supervisory position or both was not available, a simplified version [GOLDCLASS_SIMP, NSSEC_SIMP] has been created based on detailed occupation (SOC90) only. For each job spell a mixed version [GOLDCLASS_MIXED, NSSEC_MIXED] of the classifications has been constructed, which takes the value of the full version, if it is available, otherwise takes the value of the simplified version.
