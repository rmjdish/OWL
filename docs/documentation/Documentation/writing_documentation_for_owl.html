Writing documentation for OWL

All datasets and derived variables sent to NSHD should be accompanied by a completed topsheet — it's how the work behind them gets recorded. Once you've filled one in and sent it to us, it's turned automatically into a page on OWL and a matching downloadable PDF, making your documentation part of the permanent, searchable record for that variable rather than a Word file only you know exists. This page explains what that process actually involves and why it's worth taking a little care over the formatting; the downloadable guide below shows you exactly how to write the document itself, using a real worked example throughout.

You don't need to learn anything new or unusual: everything in the guide uses Word's own normal features — the Styles gallery, Bold, Underline, Bullets, Numbering, and Tables — exactly the way you'd already use them. Every example in the guide is taken from one real document, referred to throughout as the Atopy example.

From Word document to webpage

OWL is NSHD's Online Wellbeing and Lifecourse resource — where researchers search and browse thousands of variables by topic, by the year they were collected, or by how often other researchers have used them, and build up a "basket" of the variables they want. Every variable in the dictionary already has its own page on OWL. What's missing, for a lot of variables, is the story behind them — how they were derived, what decisions were made, what a researcher would benefit from knowing before using it for their project. That's what your documentation adds.

Once your document is turned into a webpage, it isn't just filed away — it becomes a real, permanent part of OWL: it shows up in search results, it's linked to directly from every variable it describes, and it's easy to find for anyone browsing that topic. A copy is also generated as a downloadable PDF, available from a button at the top of the page.

The overall shape of a document: two title lines, the topsheet table, then as many sections as you need.

Because the page and the PDF are built automatically by reading the structure of your Word document — its headings, its table, its bold text — following the patterns on this page means your document can go live straight away, with nothing needing to be manually fixed or reformatted first. That's the difference between your documentation appearing on OWL the same day the data is made available, and it sitting in a queue waiting for manual corrections.

How your variables get found and displayed

Everything in this section describes what appears on the OWL webpage once your document has been processed — not something you need to build or format inside the Word document itself. The variable names in your document don't need to be marked up or tagged in any special way — they're found automatically. But how a variable name appears in your document changes how confidently it gets linked, and what a researcher can do with it once your page is live.

There are four ways a variable name gets recognised. Listed in the Output variables field of the topsheet table is the strongest, most deliberate way — you're explicitly stating "this document is about these variables." Written as a bold heading on its own line is just as reliable, since it clearly marks that variable as the subject of a section. Following a sweep-year pattern like HTyyU alongside a list of years is equally reliable, and gets automatically expanded into every year's version of that variable. Mentioned anywhere else, in ordinary sentences, is still picked up automatically, but treated more cautiously. If you want a variable clearly and reliably linked, put it in the Output variables field or give it its own bold heading — don't just leave it to be found by chance somewhere in a sentence.

On the page itself, under "Variables in this document", every variable found is sorted into one of three tables. High confidence variables — named via the Output variables field, a bold heading, or a sweep-year template — are shown expanded, with an "Add all to basket" button so every one can be added in a single click. Medium confidence variables — mentioned in ordinary text with a longer, distinctive name (5 or more letters) — are likely relevant, but shown collapsed and added one at a time. Low confidence variables — mentioned in ordinary text with a short name (4 letters or fewer) — need a closer look before adding, since a short word can coincidentally match a real variable code without the sentence actually being about it.

Matching isn't case-sensitive — HTYYU, htyyu, and HtYyU all match the same variable — though since OWL and CONDOR always display variable names in lowercase, it's best practice to write them in lowercase too, so nothing looks visually mismatched. Every variable listed under "Variables in this document" has a checkbox next to it, letting anyone viewing the page tick it to add that variable straight to their OWL basket — or, for high-confidence variables, add the whole tier in one click. Good documentation doesn't just explain a variable — formatted well, it makes that variable one click away from being requested by the next researcher who needs it.

The topsheet table

At the top of your document, after the two standard title lines ("CLEANED/DERIVED VARIABLE METADATA TOP SHEET" and "For Submission to the NSHD Scientific Support Team"), fill in the topsheet table exactly as you always have. Nothing about how you fill it in needs to change.

Behind the scenes, only five of these fields are ever shown publicly on the page — Categories of variables, Summary of work undertaken, Names of source variables, Output variables, and Papers using these variables. Everything else — file locations, dates, syntax details — is used internally and automatically kept out of anything published. You don't need to worry about which is which; it's handled for you either way. If a field genuinely doesn't apply, it's fine to leave it blank or write "N/A" — blank fields simply don't appear on the page rather than showing up as an empty row.

Sections, headings, and subheadings

After the table, write your document in as many sections as make sense for what you're describing. Each section title should use the Heading 1 style from Word's Styles gallery — that's the single thing that tells the page where one section ends and the next begins. There's no fixed number of sections you need, and no required titles.

Within a section, you can break things up further with a subheading. Four options all work, and all display consistently, so use whichever feels most natural as you write: a real Heading 2 style, text formatted as both Bold and Underlined, text formatted as Underlined only, or text formatted as Bold only. A subheading should be its own short line, not the start of a sentence that carries straight on.

Lists, tables, and images

Use Word's own Bullets and Numbering buttons as normal — they carry through exactly as you'd expect. One thing worth knowing: a numbered list with only a single item in it will be treated as a heading instead of a list, since a genuine numbered list almost never has just one entry — this is nearly always what's actually meant (for example, a stray "1." left over from copying text between documents, in front of what's really a bold heading).

Any table you insert into your text — separate from the topsheet table at the top — is treated as ordinary content and displayed as a proper table on the page and in the PDF. Pictures, charts, or diagrams placed anywhere in your document appear in the same spot on the page and in the PDF, exactly where you placed them in Word — nothing extra is needed to include them.

Putting it all together

The downloadable guide walks through a full worked example — the Atopy example — showing every one of these features used together: two bold+underlined title lines, the topsheet table, then three sections each using a different mix of subheadings, lists, a content table, and an embedded image. Your own document doesn't need to include every single feature — that example simply gathers everything that's possible into one place so you can see it in context.

The guide ends with a genuine blank template: the two title lines, a full topsheet table with placeholder text shown in grey italics, and two placeholder sections ready to be replaced with your own content. Copy it into a new Word document, replace the placeholders, and it's ready to send to us.
