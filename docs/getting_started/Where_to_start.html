Where should I start?

Two approaches, five tools — pick the one that fits how you think about the problem.

On this page

  • Search Data Dictionary
  • Browse by Category
  • Popular Variables
  • NSHD Questionnaires
  • Topics
Back to getting started

The OWL splits broadly into two approaches. Search methods are for when you already have something concrete to look for — a variable name, a keyword, or a general category you can narrow into. Explore documentation is for when you're browsing without a specific target in mind, working from a survey wave or a subject area outward toward the variables themselves. Most research workflows end up using a mix of both, often starting broad with documentation and finishing precise with search. The five sections below cover each tool in turn, grouped by which of the two approaches they belong to.

Search methods

Search Data Dictionary

The Data Dictionary Search is the most direct route into the dataset when you already have something concrete to search for — a variable name, a keyword from a label, or a phrase you remember from a description. It searches variable names, labels, descriptions, and category metadata simultaneously, updating live as you type, so you can see results narrowing in real time rather than waiting for a page reload.

What makes it particularly powerful is the accompanying filter panel, which lets you layer additional constraints on top of a text search — restricting results further. This combination of free-text search and structured filtering means you can start broad and narrow down quickly, without needing to know the exact spelling or format of a variable name in advance.

This is the right tool if you've read a publication or report that references a specific variable, if a colleague has mentioned a measure by name, or if you already roughly know what you're after and just need to confirm the variable coding, sweep, or label wording before using it in your own analysis.

Browse by Category

Browse by Category presents the entire dataset as a structured, expandable tree — broad domains like clinical data, biological samples, or questionnaire data sit at the top, branching down through progressively narrower sub-categories until you reach individual variables. Unlike a free-text search, this method doesn't require you to guess a keyword at all; instead you drill down step by step, narrowing from a general area into specific measures.

Each level of the tree carries its own filterable, sortable table, so once you've drilled into a sub-category you can still search and filter within it, combining the structure of browsing with the precision of search at the point where it's most useful. Categories with available data are marked distinctly from organisational placeholder categories, so you can tell at a glance which branches actually lead to variables versus which are purely structural groupings.

This is especially useful when you're unfamiliar with how the dataset is organised, when you want to see everything available within a domain rather than a single matching result, or when a category name is more memorable to you than any specific variable name within it.

View Popular Variables

Popular Variables takes a different approach entirely: rather than searching or browsing by subject, it shows you what other researchers have actually used. The table tracks how frequently each variable has been added to project baskets across recent research activity, giving a practical signal of which measures the wider research community considers essential, well-validated, or simply convenient for common types of analysis.

This is a useful complement to the other methods rather than a replacement for them — it works well as a sanity check once you've identified a candidate set of variables, letting you see whether your selections align with common practice, or whether there's a frequently-used alternative measure you hadn't considered. It's equally useful at the very start of a project, when you want a quick sense of established, frequently-used measures in a general area before diving into a more targeted search.

The table includes usage counts, full variable labels, and direct links through to each variable's metadata page.

Explore documentation

NSHD Questionnaires

The Questionnaires archive preserves the original chronological structure of the study, organised sweep by sweep from the 1946 maternity survey through to the most recent data collection. This is the place to go when you want to see exactly how a question was worded, what response options were offered, or which respondent — the study member themselves, a parent, or a teacher — actually answered a given question.

Each questionnaire page presents a downloadable copy of the original survey instrument as it was actually administered, with variable names annotated directly onto the PDF wherever possible — so you can trace a specific item in the data dictionary back to the precise question that generated it, in its original context and phrasing. This is invaluable for understanding subtle changes in question wording or response scales between sweeps, which can materially affect how comparable a variable is across different ages.

Researchers often turn to this archive when preparing a methods section, checking for measurement consistency across waves, or simply trying to understand what a study member was actually being asked at a particular point in their life, rather than relying solely on the abbreviated variable label.

Topics

Topics is built for understanding a subject area, not primarily for finding individual variables — it's the right starting point when you want context on how something was measured across the study, before you go looking for the data itself. Variables are grouped thematically into areas like physical health, cognition, mental wellbeing, and lifestyle, with each topic page explaining what was measured, how, and across which sweeps.

Most topic pages are documentation rather than data listings: they describe the instruments used, summarise how measurement changed over time, and link out to the relevant questionnaires or variable metadata pages rather than presenting variables directly. A smaller number of topic pages — typically ones built around a single well-defined questionnaire, like the MRC respiratory symptom questionnaire — do include a table of the actual variable names across sweeps, but this is the exception rather than the rule. Because the NSHD has run for nearly 80 years, a topic can span multiple decades and several different instruments or measurement approaches, and the topic page's main job is to make that history legible in one place.

Use Topics when you want to understand a subject's coverage and measurement history before diving into variables, or when you're scoping out what's available in a general area. If you need an actual variable list, Browse by Category or Search Data Dictionary will usually get you there faster — Topics will often point you toward the right questionnaire or category to search next, rather than listing variables itself.
