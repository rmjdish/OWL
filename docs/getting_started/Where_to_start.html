Where should I start?

What you need depends on what you already know — here's a guide to picking the right tool.

On this page

  • Data Dictionary
  • Topics
  • Questionnaires
  • Popular variables
Back to getting started

The OWL offers several ways to find what you need, and none of them is the single "right" one — which to use depends on whether you already have a specific variable in mind, a general topic you're exploring, or a survey wave you want to revisit. The four sections below walk through each method and when it's the best fit.

Data Dictionary Search

The Data Dictionary Search is the most direct route into the dataset when you already have something concrete to search for — a variable name, a keyword from a label, or a phrase you remember from a description. It searches variable names, labels, descriptions, and category metadata simultaneously, updating live as you type, so you can see results narrowing in real time rather than waiting for a page reload.

What makes it particularly powerful is the accompanying filter panel, which lets you layer additional constraints on top of a text search — restricting results to a specific sweep, category, or data type. This combination of free-text search and structured filtering means you can start broad and narrow down quickly, without needing to know the exact spelling or format of a variable name in advance.

This is the right tool if you've read a publication or report that references a specific variable, if a colleague has mentioned a measure by name, or if you already roughly know what you're after and just need to confirm the exact coding, sweep coverage, or label wording before using it in your own analysis.

Topics

Topics is built for browsing rather than searching — it's the right starting point when you know the subject area you're interested in but don't yet know which variables, sweeps, or instruments are relevant. Variables are grouped thematically into areas like physical health, cognition, mental wellbeing, and lifestyle, with each topic page giving an overview of what was measured, how, and across which ages.

Because the NSHD has run for nearly 80 years, many topics span multiple decades and several different instruments or measurement approaches. A topic page on respiratory health, for instance, draws together the MRC respiratory questionnaire data collected at nine separate sweeps from age 20 through to age 76, alongside lung function measurements that were introduced later in the study and measured using different equipment over time. The topic page lays all of this out together, so you can see the full longitudinal picture of a subject area in one place rather than piecing it together sweep by sweep.

This approach particularly suits exploratory work — situations where you're scoping out what's available before committing to a specific analysis plan, or where you want to discover related variables you might not have thought to search for directly by name.

Questionnaires

The Questionnaires archive preserves the original chronological structure of the study, organised sweep by sweep from the 1946 maternity survey through to the most recent data collection. This is the place to go when you want to see exactly how a question was worded, what response options were offered, or which respondent (the study member themselves, a parent, or a teacher) actually answered a given question.

Each questionnaire page presents a downloadable copy of the original survey instrument as it was actually administered, with variable names annotated directly onto the PDF wherever possible — so you can trace a specific item in the data dictionary back to the precise question that generated it, in its original context and phrasing. This is invaluable for understanding subtle changes in question wording or response scales between sweeps, which can materially affect how comparable a variable is across different ages.

Researchers often turn to this archive when preparing a methods section, checking for measurement consistency across waves, or simply trying to understand what a study member was actually being asked at a particular point in their life, rather than relying solely on the abbreviated variable label.

Popular variables

Popular Variables takes a different approach entirely: rather than searching or browsing by subject, it shows you what other researchers have actually used. The table tracks how frequently each variable has been added to project baskets across recent research activity, giving a practical signal of which measures the wider research community considers essential, well-validated, or simply convenient for common types of analysis.

This is a useful complement to the other three methods rather than a replacement for them — it works well as a sanity check once you've identified a candidate set of variables, letting you see whether your selections align with common practice, or whether there's a frequently-used alternative measure you hadn't considered. It's equally useful at the very start of a project, when you want a quick sense of established, frequently-used measures in a general area before diving into a more targeted search.

The table includes usage counts, full variable labels, and direct links through to each variable's metadata page, and can be sorted or filtered to focus on a particular area of interest, making it easy to compare popularity across related variables at a glance.
